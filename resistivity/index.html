Electrical Resistivity Surveys (VES) in Ottawa: Subsurface Mapping Without Drilling

Rigorous testing. Clear reporting.

LEARN MORE

When the Ontario Building Code references geotechnical investigation, most people think of drill rigs. But in Ottawa, where the sensitive Leda clay and buried bedrock valleys create a complex subsurface, drilling alone often misses the lateral picture. ASTM D6431 provides a clear framework for vertical electrical sounding, and our lab runs these surveys to map stratigraphy without breaking ground. The method sends DC current into the soil and measures apparent resistivity—useful for locating groundwater, outlining clay pockets, or identifying granular lenses that could act as drainage paths. In a city built on post-glacial sediments, combining resistivity data with SPT drilling gives you both the vertical profile and the lateral continuity that borehole logs alone can’t confirm. For linear infrastructure like the O-Train extensions or pipeline corridors along the Rideau River, this approach reduces the number of invasive tests while improving the geological model.

Resistivity data turns a scattered set of boreholes into a continuous geological model, showing where the Leda clay thins or the bedrock drops.

Scope of work

A common mistake on Ottawa construction sites is assuming that a few boreholes spaced 50 meters apart can capture the abrupt transitions in Champlain Sea deposits. We’ve seen projects where a thin layer of sensitive clay went undetected between boreholes, leading to differential settlement and costly underpinning later. The VES method helps close that gap. Our field crew uses a Schlumberger array with expanding electrode spacing, sometimes pushing current electrodes beyond 100 meters to reach the limestone bedrock. Data inversion is performed with the same software we use for seismic refraction surveys, which allows joint interpretation when both resistivity and velocity models are available. The output is a calibrated resistivity cross-section, usually correlated to lab grain-size analysis of existing samples. For sites near the Greenbelt with variable overburden thickness, this approach prevents surprises during excavation and shoring design.

Area-specific notes

Ottawa’s freeze-thaw cycle creates a particular challenge for electrical resistivity work. From November through March, the top 80 to 120 centimeters of soil can freeze solid, acting as an insulating cap that forces current to find deeper, unpredictable paths. Running a VES survey on frozen ground without accounting for this almost guarantees distorted resistivity values and a false bedrock signature. We schedule surveys between April and October whenever possible, and when winter work is unavoidable, we pre-wet electrode positions with a saline solution and take shallow temperature profiles to correct the inversion model. Spring melt is another problem: saturated surface soils after the snowpack drains can mask the true resistivity contrast between clay and silt. The key is not just collecting data, but knowing when the ground conditions will give you reliable numbers.

Common questions

How much does a VES electrical resistivity survey cost in Ottawa?

For a standard vertical electrical sounding with full data processing and a report, rates typically fall between CA$960 and CA$1,470 per sounding line. The final cost depends on access conditions, the number of stations, and whether we need to integrate the results with existing lab data or other geophysical datasets.

Can electrical resistivity distinguish between Leda clay and glacial till?

Yes, usually. Leda clay typically shows resistivity between 5 and 30 Ω·m, while the underlying glacial till reads much higher—often 40 to 150 Ω·m depending on silt content and moisture. The contrast is sharp enough that a well-calibrated VES profile can map the clay-till interface across a site, which is critical for slope stability analysis in the Ottawa Valley.

What depth can a VES survey reach in Ottawa soils?

With a Schlumberger array and maximum current-electrode spacing of 300 meters, we regularly reach investigation depths between 60 and 120 meters. That’s sufficient to penetrate the entire Quaternary overburden and image the top of the Paleozoic limestone bedrock, even in deep channels south of the Ottawa River.

Is a resistivity survey enough on its own for foundation design?

No, resistivity is a screening and correlation tool. It gives you the lateral picture, but you still need direct physical data—borehole logs, SPT N-values, and lab index testing—to calibrate the geophysical model. We always recommend pairing VES profiles with a targeted drilling program so the resistivity boundaries are tied to real soil units.
